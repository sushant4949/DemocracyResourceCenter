Written by 299 people, over 165 days, for the India we live in today

India had never held a universal-franchise election when it sat down to write the longest constitution in the world. This is how it happened, who was in the room, and how the text has changed since.

The six Fundamental Rights

The Constitution gives us six Fundamental Rights. Think of them as six guarantees that help every person live with freedom, equality and dignity.

01

Right to Equality

Articles 14 to 18

Same rules. Same respect.

No matter who you are, the law must treat you equally. Your caste, religion, gender or place of birth cannot be used to unfairly discriminate against you. The Constitution also abolishes untouchability and prohibits the State from granting certain titles.

In everyday lifeEveryone deserves the same respect, whether they are sitting in the front row or the last row.

02

Right to Freedom

Articles 19 to 22

Speak your mind. Move freely. Choose your path.

You have the freedom to express your views, gather peacefully, form associations, travel across India, live where you choose and choose your work. These Articles also protect people against certain forms of unfair punishment, protect life and personal liberty, and provide safeguards against arbitrary arrest and detention. Article 21A also guarantees free and compulsory education for children between six and fourteen years of age.

In everyday lifeYou can share an idea, travel to another state, choose your work and expect your life and liberty to be protected.

03

Right against Exploitation

Articles 23 to 24

Nobody gets to use you unfairly.

No one can force you into bonded or forced labour. Human trafficking is prohibited, and children below fourteen cannot be employed in factories, mines or other hazardous work.

In everyday lifeYour time, work and safety matter. Nobody has the right to make you work through force or exploitation.

04

Right to Freedom of Religion

Articles 25 to 28

Believe. Question. Choose.

You have the freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate your religion, subject to constitutional limits such as public order, morality and health. The Constitution also protects certain freedoms related to managing religious affairs and religious instruction.

In everyday lifeWhat you believe, how you practise your faith and whether you choose to have a faith are personal matters protected by the Constitution.

05

Cultural and Educational Rights

Articles 29 to 30

Your language. Your culture. Your story.

India is home to many languages, cultures and traditions. These rights protect the ability of communities to conserve their distinct language, script and culture. They also protect the right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

In everyday lifeThe language you speak at home, the culture you grow up with and the stories your community carries are part of what makes India diverse.

06

Right to Constitutional Remedies

Article 32

When your rights are in trouble, you have a way to fight back.

Fundamental Rights are not just promises on paper. If your Fundamental Rights are violated, Article 32 allows you to approach the Supreme Court for their enforcement. High Courts also have the power to protect Fundamental Rights under Article 226.

In everyday lifeIf your Fundamental Rights are violated, the Constitution gives you a way to ask the courts to step in and protect them.

One Constitution. Six guarantees.

These rights are not just big words from a textbook. They shape everyday life: who gets heard, who gets treated equally, who gets to make choices and what happens when those rights are denied.

They are some of the ways the Constitution turns the ideas of justice, liberty, equality and dignity into something we can experience in our everyday lives.

The limit Parliament cannot cross

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a bench of thirteen judges held that Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution but may not damage or destroy its basic structure — its supremacy, the republican and democratic form of government, secularism, the separation of powers, judicial review and the rule of law. Every amendment since has been written in the shadow of that ruling.
